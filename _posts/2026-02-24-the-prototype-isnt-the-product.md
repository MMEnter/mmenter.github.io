This evening in VS Code I opened a brand‑new file and asked GitHub Copilot to help me outline my LinkedIn post for this week. I had just created my own skill—`merlinslinkedineditor`—to make sure my posts carry my voice while getting the polish I’m not great at. I usually start writing these posts myself and then use tools to rewrite and refine. Today I felt a bit lazy and decided to let Copilot take the yoke and write the first draft.

When I reviewed the draft, I was blown away! It decided to write about how I write my posts, which was exactly what I actually wanted to write about. I was so shocked that I immediately messaged a colleague, telling him that it was my turn to freak out about AI. But then I started reading the post and realized that it completely missed my style, it didn’t sound like me at all. It was a good reminder that while AI can create something that looks impressive at first glance, it is far from the done product.

So, I scrapped it and wrote the post myself.

That experience reminded me of something I bump into all the time at work. The barrier to producing “something that works” keeps getting lower. Which is amazing for prototyping and exploring ideas, but it can be a trap if you or your stakeholders forget that the prototype isn’t the product. LinkedIn and our news cycles are filled with stories of fast paced development and impressive demos, but the real work is still in the details.

At first glance it looks like something is the right solution. But experience teaches you to look for the gaps, there are edge cases you didn’t anticipate, assumptions that break under load, next steps you underestimated. We forget that these prototypes are not the done product. The gap between "it works" and it is working is still wide. Turning that idea into something reliable takes more than a coding agent it takes the know‑how you only get after building the same thing wrong a dozen times.
